Minor Repair Clause

Who pays when something breaks?

Important: This guide is for general information, not legal, tax or professional advice. Rules may change, and your signed agreement and circumstances matter. Check official sources before acting. Read the full disclaimer →
IN PLAIN ENGLISH

A minor-repair clause allocates certain repair costs between landlord and tenant. The amount and scope are negotiated contract terms, not a single fixed Singapore-wide legal repair cap.

Sources checked: 9 October 2026 · Singapore

01

Start with the cause

Is the issue ordinary wear and tear, an existing defect, structural failure, poor maintenance or tenant misuse? A contractor's written diagnosis can help where the cause is disputed.

02

Read the actual repair cap

Some agreements set a maximum contribution per repair or item. Check whether the cap applies to labour, parts, replacements or specific appliances, and whether there are exclusions.

03

Check special clauses

Blocked drains, aircon maintenance, leaks and major replacements may have their own clauses. An agreed one-month defects period may affect who handles pre-existing issues, but it is not a universal statutory warranty.

04

Before authorising repairs

Notify the landlord or managing agent promptly, keep photos and get approval where required. For emergencies, document the issue and actions taken, and prioritise safety.

REAL-WORLD RENTAL SCENARIO

The cooker hood stops working. Must the tenant automatically pay the first $300?

Imagine the hood buttons were repaired before move-in, but the fault returns during the lease. The tenant says it was already defective; the landlord points to a $300 minor-repair clause. Both facts may matter, but neither automatically settles who pays.

What to do next

  1. Check the exact clause: is the cap per item, incident or repair, and are existing defects excluded?
  2. Compare the move-in inventory, earlier repair messages and dated photos.
  3. Get a contractor to identify the likely cause and whether repair or replacement is sensible.
  4. Discuss a proportionate resolution in writing before deducting or paying anything.

JC’s practical takeaway: A $300 figure is an example of a negotiated clause, not a Singapore-wide legal rule.

VISUAL EXAMPLE · ILLUSTRATION ONLY

How a S$300 repair contribution might work

Assume the signed agreement says the tenant pays the first S$300 of an eligible repair and the landlord pays the balance. This is an example of one possible contract wording, not a rule for all tenancies.

Illustrative repair bill: S$480
Tenant S$300
Landlord S$180

Important: This split applies only if the particular fault is covered by that clause. Pre-existing defects, misuse, exclusions, replacement rules or a different contract definition can change the outcome.

Before agreeing to pay

  1. Ask for the contractor’s diagnosis and an itemised quotation.
  2. Check the signed repair clause and any handover defect records.
  3. Confirm approval and the agreed cost split in writing.
JC’S PRACTICAL TIP

Separate the question 'Who caused it?' from 'Who pays under this particular clause?' That distinction prevents many repair disputes.

PUT THIS GUIDE INTO PRACTICE

Before agreeing to a repair bill

Illustrative scenario: A recurring appliance fault

A dishwasher fails again after an earlier repair. Keep the earlier job sheet: the date a fault returns does not, by itself, establish its cause or who must pay.

Who should do what?

  • Tenant: report the symptoms promptly, with photos and previous repair messages. Avoid authorising non-urgent work before the required approval.
  • Landlord: check the earlier repair history and ask for a diagnosis before deciding on a contribution.
  • Both: record the applicable clause, approved quotation and agreed allocation before work starts.
